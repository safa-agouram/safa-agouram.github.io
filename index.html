Mapping Dead Neurons After ReLU

Deep networks used to read mammograms are, to their own designers, largely opaque. We built a generalist diagnostic tool that takes any PyTorch model and any dataloader and returns a map of which neurons never fire — then pointed it at GMIC, a published, radiologist-competitive breast-cancer screening model, to see what its blind spots look like.

01 — Context

AI is already reading mammograms. It rarely explains itself.

Today, deep neural networks are widely used for image analysis, but their inner workings remain difficult to interpret. In networks using the ReLU activation function, certain neurons can become inactive (a phenomenon known as the dying ReLU problem) which can impair the network's learning capacity. Several recent studies examine this phenomenon and aim to mitigate it or reactivate these neurons.

02 — Study objective

In this context, monitoring internal activations provides an approach to better understanding network behavior. This work thus aims to develop a tool for visualizing network architecture and pinpointing layers containing inactive neurons through activation analysis. The approach is first evaluated on a simple CNN architecture, and then on a more complex convolutional network (the GMIC architecture) used for mammography analysis in the context of breast cancer prediction.

Keywords

CNN, Dying neurons, ReLU

02 — Why map dead neurons

A cartography of what the network stopped using

Interpretability research on medical imaging models has mostly focused on where a network is looking — saliency maps, attention overlays, Grad-CAM heatmaps drawn on top of the mammogram itself. That work matters, but there is a second, orthogonal question that saliency maps do not answer: not where the network looks, but which of its internal units are doing any work at all.

03 — A generalist mapping tool

From activation to visualization

To analyze the internal behavior of a network, data is propagated through the model. Hooks then retrieve the internal activations of the layers under study. These activations are analyzed to identify neurons that produce no positive activation across the observed examples, and this information is then linked to a visual representation of the architecture.

04 — Case study I

SmallCNN: validating the visualization pipeline

To validate the proposed approach, we first applied our visualization pipeline to a simple convolutional neural network (SmallCNN). After propagating the input data through the network, forward hooks recorded the activations of each layer during inference. These activations were then analyzed to identify neurons that never produced a positive activation across the entire dataset.

The resulting visualization is shown below.

This first experiment demonstrates that the proposed method successfully links the internal activations collected through forward hooks to a graphical representation of the network architecture. Although performed on a simple model, it validates the complete visualization pipeline and confirms that inactive neurons can be automatically identified and localized within the network. This proof of concept provides the basis for applying the same methodology to more complex architectures, such as GMIC.
