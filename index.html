Mapping Dead Neurons After ReLU

Convolutional neural networks have become widely used for image analysis, including medical imaging applications. However, despite their strong predictive performance, understanding their internal behaviour remains a challenging task.

01 — Why examining dead ReLu?

Context

Convolutional neural networks are heavily over-parameterised: the number of trainable weights routinely exceeds the number of training samples, which gives them a strong tendency to memorise rather than generalise on supervised tasks such as classification. The usual remedy is data. For the empirical risk minimiser (ERM) to generalise — that is, for its true risk under the underlying distribution P to stay close to the empirical risk it was trained to minimise — one draws a training set from P large enough for the two to coincide. In medical imaging that remedy is often unavailable: ground-truth labels require expert annotation and, for endpoints such as biopsy-confirmed malignancy, are both expensive and technically difficult to obtain. When the sample size cannot be increased, the alternative is to reduce the number of parameters to what the task actually requires — parsimony instead of more data. Identifying the parts of a CNN that play no role in the task, in the sense that their ReLU output never leaves zero, is one concrete route: those branches carry weights that are trained but never used, and could in principle be removed. This is the dying ReLU problem, and it costs more than wasted capacity — a unit whose pre-activation is negative for every input receives no gradient, so it cannot recover on its own, and the network's effective learning capacity shrinks accordingly. Several recent studies analyse the phenomenon and propose ways to mitigate it or to bring the affected units back to life.

Related work

Work on the dying ReLU falls into three broad families, following the taxonomy proposed by Lu et al. (2020). The first modifies the architecture, and in particular the activation function: leaky ReLU (Maas et al., 2013), ELU (Clevert et al., 2016), SELU (Klambauer et al., 2017) and searched activations (Ramachandran et al., 2017) all preserve some gradient signal on the negative side, although their relative benefit varies across tasks and datasets and each adds at least one hyper-parameter to tune. The second inserts extra operations into the training loop — batch normalisation (Ioffe and Szegedy, 2015), dropout (Srivastava et al., 2014) — at the cost of additional computation per iteration. The third leaves both the architecture and the training procedure untouched and changes only how weights and biases are initialised (Glorot and Bengio, 2010; Saxe et al., 2014; He et al., 2015; Mishkin and Matas, 2016). Lu et al. (2020) give the first theoretical treatment of the problem in this third setting. They prove that under the symmetric, zero-centred distributions used by essentially all standard schemes, the probability that a ReLU network is born dead — already a constant function at initialisation, and therefore beyond rescue by any gradient-based optimiser — converges to one as depth grows, while at fixed depth it vanishes as width grows. Death is thus a property of the architecture as much as of the initialiser, which explains both why deep narrow networks are hard to train and why the wide networks used in practice rarely collapse outright. Their proposed randomised asymmetric initialisation (RAI) draws a single, randomly chosen entry of each weight-and-bias row from a positive, asymmetric distribution and the remaining entries from a centred Gaussian, with the parameters fixed by a second-moment analysis so as not to trade the dying ReLU for exploding activations; the resulting born-dead probability is substantially lower than under He initialisation. Shin and Karniadakis (2020) move from whole-network death to the number of dead units, distinguish tentative from permanent death, and treat trainability — having few enough permanently dead units for the task at hand — as an upper bound on the success rate of training, which motivates a data-dependent initialisation. More recent work intervenes during training rather than before it: Cai et al. (2021) route the negative phase through a parallel trainable branch instead of discarding it, reconstructing units that a plain ReLU would have killed, while Horuz et al. (2025) apply surrogate gradients to inactive ReLU units (SUGAR) so that they keep receiving updates — a plug-in regulariser that leaves the architecture unchanged. What all of these approaches presuppose is that one already knows where in the network the problem is. To the best of our knowledge, no simple tool exists for visualising which parts of an architecture never activate across a full forward pass over an empirical dataset — for seeing where the dying ReLU actually occurs in a specific network, and how much of it is affected. That is our contribution.

Our contributions

  • Given a PyTorch model and a PyTorch DataLoader, a user-friendly tool that maps and visualises dead ReLU units across every sample in the loader.
  • Two case studies in supervised learning: object classification on CIFAR-10 (Krizhevsky, 2009) with a small CNN, and detection of malignant patterns in mammograms with GMIC (Shen et al., 2021), a recent multiple-instance-learning CNN architecture. The complete implementation is available on our GitHub repository.

    • 02 — A generalist mapping tool

    From activation to visualization

    To analyze the internal behavior of a network, data is propagated through the model. Hooks then retrieve the internal activations of the layers under study. These activations are analyzed to identify neurons that produce no positive activation across the observed examples, and this information is then linked to a visual representation of the architecture.

    Dead neuron criterion

    Let \(a_i^{(k)}(x)\) denote the post-ReLU activation of neuron \(i\) in layer \(k\) for an input image \(x \in \mathcal{D}\). A neuron is considered dead if its activation remains non-positive for every image in the dataset. We define the dead-neuron indicator as:

    \[ d_i^{(k)} = \mathbf{1}\!\left( \forall x \in \mathcal{D}, \; a_i^{(k)}(x)\le 0 \right) \]

    where \(\mathbf{1}(\cdot)\) denotes the indicator function.

    03 — Case study : SmallCNN architectur

    As an initial validation of the proposed visualization pipeline, we applied our method to a lightweight convolutional neural network (SmallCNN). The architecture consists of two convolutional layers, each followed by a ReLU activation function and a max-pooling operation, and two fully connected layers with a ReLU activation after the first fully connected layer. The network was trained on a binary classification task derived from the CIFAR-10 dataset (cat vs. dog) for 10 epochs using a learning rate of 1 × 10−4. The best-performing checkpoint, achieving a test accuracy close to 70%, was selected for all subsequent visualization experiments. The resulting architecture is illustrated below.

    Observation: no dead neurons were found in the convolutional layers; 18 of the 64 neurons in fc1 (28.1%) never activate. This first, controlled case shows that dead units are not evenly distributed across a network — a question we now examine at a much larger scale with GMIC.

    04 — Case study : GMIC model

    Following the validation of the visualization pipeline on SmallCNN, the method was applied to the GMIC model. GMIC is a multiple-instance-learning CNN for high-resolution breast-cancer screening images, using the model and weights released in the authors' repository, nyukat/GMIC. This model is a more complex architecture combining multiple components, including a global network based on DSNet, a local detection network using ResNet blocks, a MIL attention module, and a fusion branch for the final classification. No fine-tuning was performed for this model, and the pretrained GMIC architecture was directly used for the visualization experiments. The goal is to identify the areas of the network exhibiting low neuronal activity. The obtained results were directly integrated into the model's graphical representation, highlighting in red the layers containing inactive neurons, along with the corresponding number and percentage of affected neurons. This visualization provides a global overview of the activation distribution across the different branches of the model (global, local, and fusion branches).

    The study of dead neurons provides a better understanding of which parts of the network actually contribute to image analysis, particularly in the layers responsible for extracting visual features, where inactive neurons may indicate a loss of model capacity. Observation: about a third of GMIC's convolutional and linear neurons (5.4M of 16.2M, ≈33%) never activate on our sample, concentrated overwhelmingly in the global network (86% of all dead neurons) versus the local network (14%).

    05 — Conclusion

    Interpreting the results

    If the same regions consistently appear inactive across several independent training runs and data splits, the finding is likely to reflect a structural property of the model rather than the outcome of a particular initialization. In such cases, the visualization can guide subsequent interventions proposed in the literature, including alternative activation functions, improved initialization strategies, or methods that preserve gradient flow through inactive neurons. Conversely, if inactive regions vary substantially between runs, they are more likely to result from initialization effects, making retraining or reinitialization preferable to architectural redesign.

    On SmallCNN, dead units were observed exclusively in the fully connected layer fc1 (between 6 and 18 neurons out of 64, depending on the training run), while the convolutional layers conv1 and conv2 exhibited no dead units. This distribution agrees with previous observations that convolutional feature extractors are less prone to permanently inactive neurons because they process diverse spatial patterns, whereas fully connected layers operate on compressed feature representations where redundancy is lower.

    On GMIC, inactive neurons were detected across several branches of the architecture, with higher concentrations in deeper layers. This observation is also consistent with previous studies showing that the dying ReLU phenomenon tends to become more pronounced as network depth increases. Overall, the moderate dead-unit rates observed in SmallCNN and the more widespread inactivity found in GMIC support the idea that architectural depth and complexity amplify the phenomenon. These findings further motivate the use of the proposed visualization tool as a diagnostic aid for deep neural networks, where identifying inactive regions can provide valuable insight into model behaviour before considering pruning or architectural modifications.

    Concluding remarks

    Finding and visualising dead units is a diagnostic step. Acting on this diagnosis, whether by changing the activation function or pruning inactive units to obtain a more parsimonious model, is a separate task. The dead-unit rates reported here are empirical: they are measured on a single set of weights, obtained from one stochastic gradient descent training run and evaluated on a finite dataset. Demonstrating that a dead region reflects the architecture and the task, rather than a particular training instance, requires additional validation.

    Future work
    • Cross-validation of dead-unit maps by retraining the network on multiple train/test splits and recomputing activation maps on held-out data.
    • A systematic comparison of initialization strategies, since initialization is known to play a major role in the dying ReLU phenomenon. (Lu et al., 2020; Shin and Karniadakis, 2020).
